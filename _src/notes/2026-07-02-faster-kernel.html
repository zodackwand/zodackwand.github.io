title: Two weeks on a kernel, beaten by a library description: I wrote a fused kernel. The vendor library was faster. ---

The profile said sixty-one per cent of the step was in one fused block. The arithmetic said that block moved four times more memory than it needed to. I spent two weeks on the gap between those two numbers.

The napkin version, before any code:

tokens      = 8192
hidden      = 4096
bytes/elem  = 2            (bf16)
round trips = 4            (what the naive version does)

traffic = 8192 * 4096 * 2 * 4  =  268 MB
at ~2.6 TB/s                   =  103 us

measured                       =  390 us

A factor of about four. Gaps that size are wide enough to look like one clever rewrite away, and are usually made of six dull things instead.

What happened

I got the kernel to 190 µs. Then I benchmarked the vendor library, which I had skipped at the start because I assumed it did not fuse this pattern. It does, as of a version whose release notes I had not read. It runs in 171 µs. Two weeks of work produced something ten per cent slower than one line of somebody else's code.

What was left over

Half the speedup came from a layout change upstream of the kernel rather than from the kernel itself. That change stayed, and it helps the library version too.

The benchmark harness turned out to be measuring with the cache warm in a way production never is, which had been quietly flattering every result it produced for months. Finding that was worth more than the kernel would have been.

And I now know the shape of the roofline for this operator, so the next time a profile points here the decision takes ten minutes rather than ten days.

The rule I took away is to benchmark the boring option first. Knowing where it lands is what tells you how much room the interesting option has.
