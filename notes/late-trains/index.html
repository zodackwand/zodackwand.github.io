Trains, and the arithmetic of being late

19 May 2026
Home Notes Research

Two railways can advertise the same average delay and be completely different things to travel on. One is late by two minutes every day. The other is punctual for a month and then eats your evening. The mean is identical. The experience is not comparable.

I spent an afternoon on a platform in the rain working out why the timetable felt like a lie, and the answer was that I was reading a mean where I should have been reading a tail. It is the same mistake I make at work about twice a year, and it is somehow easier to see when you are the one standing outside.

The connection is the whole thing

A single late train is an inconvenience. A late train with a nine-minute connection is a two-hour problem, because the delay does not add, it multiplies through whatever comes next. The system's behaviour is set by the smallest slack anywhere in the chain, not by the average slack.

Which is why the useful question at the ticket desk is not "is this train usually on time", but "what happens if it is not". A journey with two long, forgiving changes beats a journey with one tight one, even when the tight one is forty minutes faster on paper. The forty minutes is the mean. The alternative is the tail.

The obvious analogy, stated once

Every request that fans out to eight services and waits for all of them is a nine-minute connection. You do not get the average of the eight. You get the worst of the eight, every single time, and then you get to explain to someone why the p99 does not look like the dashboard.

I will not belabour it. But I did stop optimising a median that afternoon, and I got home two hours late, which felt like the universe making the point more firmly than necessary.
