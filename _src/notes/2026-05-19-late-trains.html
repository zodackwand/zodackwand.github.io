title: Trains, and the arithmetic of being late description: Two railways, the same average delay, and completely different journeys. ---

Two railways can advertise the same average delay and be entirely different things to travel on. One is late by two minutes every day. The other is punctual for a month and then eats your evening. The mean is identical, and the mean is useless.

I worked this out on a wet platform, having chosen the faster itinerary because the timetable said it was faster. I was reading a mean where I should have been reading a tail.

The connection is the whole thing

A late train on its own is an inconvenience. A late train with a nine-minute connection is a two-hour problem, because the delay does not add to the journey so much as multiply through everything after it. What the journey feels like is set by the smallest slack anywhere in the chain, not by the average slack.

So the question at the ticket desk is not whether the train is usually on time. It is what happens when it is not. A route with two long, forgiving changes beats one with a single tight change, even when the tight one is forty minutes faster on paper. Forty minutes is the mean, and the alternative is the tail.

The same shape at work

A request that fans out to eight services and waits for all of them is a nine-minute connection. You do not get the average of the eight. You get the worst of the eight, every time, and then you get to explain why the p99 does not look like the dashboard.

I stopped optimising a median that afternoon. I also got home two hours late.
